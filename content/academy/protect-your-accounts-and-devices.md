Start with the inbox you use to reset passwords. If someone gains access to that inbox, they may be able to reset passwords elsewhere. Securing email first gives you a practical way to prioritise when a long list of accounts feels overwhelming.

## What you’ll learn

You’ll prioritise accounts, choose safer sign-in methods, keep devices updated and practise checking a suspicious message.

## Prerequisites {#what-youll-need}

Use a device you control and access to the account’s security settings. Set aside a few minutes to check your recovery details before changing how you sign in. If an account belongs to work or school, follow the organisation’s guidance for passwords, sign-in methods and reporting.

## Give each account its own password

A password reused across several services can expose all of them if any one service leaks it. A password manager can generate a long, random password for each account and store it for you. If the manager uses a master password, give it a strong, unique one and follow its recovery guidance.

Start with your email and any accounts that hold money or sensitive data. On the account’s security page, check that its recovery email or phone number still belongs to you. Then replace a reused password and save the new one in your password manager.

## Add a stronger sign-in method

Multifactor authentication asks for more than one kind of proof when you sign in. For example, after entering a password you might confirm with a code from an authenticator app. Where the service supports it, consider a passkey or security key. These methods are designed to resist phishing by tying the sign-in to the correct site.

If the site provides recovery codes, treat them like passwords and store them somewhere safe you can reach if you lose the device you usually use to sign in. Keep your current session open while you try the new sign-in method in another browser window.

## Make updates routine

Enable automatic updates where your device and apps support them, and restart when needed to finish installing them. Check the operating system, browser and applications you use regularly. A device can still turn on and run apps after its manufacturer stops providing security updates. Check the maker’s support page if you are unsure whether your model still receives fixes.

## Rehearse what you’ll do with a suspicious message

Imagine receiving an email that says your account will be closed unless you sign in immediately. Leave the message alone and open the service through its app, a saved bookmark or the address you normally use. Look for the alert there. If the claim came from someone you know, reach them through a channel you already trust.

!!! tip "An unexpected approval prompt is a reason to pause"
    If a sign-in prompt appears when you are doing something else, deny it. Open the service directly and check its recent account activity.

If you entered your password into a suspicious page, go directly to the real service to change it. Review recovery settings and active sessions, sign out sessions you cannot account for where the service allows it, and contact the provider if you lose access. If the password was reused, change it on those other services too.

## Key takeaways

Secure the email you use for password resets first. Give each account a different password, add a stronger sign-in method and keep devices updated. If a message tells you to sign in, reach the service independently.
