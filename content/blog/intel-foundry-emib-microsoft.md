Intel’s recent announcement with Microsoft puts a less familiar part of chipmaking in view. In its collaboration announcement, Intel said its Embedded Multi-die Interconnect Bridge, or EMIB, will be used to support future Azure infrastructure. The news is a concrete example of an outside customer turning to Intel for an assembly technology, alongside Intel’s effort to attract customers to its leading-edge manufacturing processes.

## The small bridge inside the package

A single chip package can contain several pieces of silicon, often called dies. A compute die might sit beside an input/output die or memory. Connecting them closely can offer more bandwidth than sending the same data over a board connection. EMIB supplies those close connections using small silicon bridges embedded in the package. Packaging therefore influences how far a hardware designer can push a multi-die system.

Manufacturing an advanced die is a separate decision. Intel’s October quarterly filing says it launched its first 18A products in high volume at the beginning of 2026 and began risk production of its 18A-P derivative in June. Risk production is a stage on the way to broader production. Intel’s agreement with Microsoft gives it a public customer story for packaging. The announcement does not establish what node every die in that future system will use or when it will ship.

## What to watch

The competitive question is whether Intel can deliver both services predictably at scale. Watch for a named product, qualification progress and volume shipments. Designers comparing suppliers need to know how long it takes to qualify a package and whether enough units can be assembled to match their plans. Those details will tell us more about Intel’s foundry position than a single customer announcement.
