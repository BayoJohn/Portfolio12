Ransomware can block access to files or systems. Some incidents also involve the theft of data. A backup can help with recovery, but it cannot take information back from someone who already copied it. That distinction matters when you are deciding how to protect files and how to respond after a suspected incident.

## Separate the backup from everyday access

Write down the documents, photos or records you cannot recreate. Keep a copy away from the computer and its everyday account if possible. An external drive disconnected after backup, or versioned storage with separately protected access, can provide an additional recovery path. Simple synchronisation may copy damaged files over useful versions. Check whether the service keeps earlier versions and how long they are available.

## Practise one restore

Pick a non-sensitive file, restore it to a temporary location and open it. Note what password, key or account you needed, and store that recovery information securely. For an organisation, check who has permission to delete backups and ensure a restore test includes at least one dependency, such as a settings file or database.

## If files suddenly become inaccessible

Stop using the affected device for sensitive work and seek help through your organisation’s approved channel if it is managed. Avoid connecting the only backup to a machine you suspect is compromised. Verify the recovery source before copying files back and use a trusted device to access important accounts. A rehearsed restore gives you a concrete next step while the incident is being assessed.
