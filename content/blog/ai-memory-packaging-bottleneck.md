A factory can make more memory chips and still fail to raise output of the AI systems that need them. High-bandwidth memory, commonly called HBM, gets its speed by combining stacked DRAM dies and placing them close to an accelerator. The stack and the finished package each have to work. Spare capacity at one stage can sit idle when another stage is full.

## One shortage, several different constraints

A memory supplier starts with dies fabricated on a wafer. It then has to assemble and test memory stacks. A separate advanced packaging step brings the memory and compute dies together. The commercial constraint is how many complete parts pass testing on time. Equipment ordered for wafer production helps at one stage, while qualified packaging capacity helps at another.

The November TechInsights report describes Samsung bringing forward a proposed start of production at an HBM packaging facility to June 2028. That is still a future date. SK hynix said in October that supply growth was struggling to match demand and projected a tight market through 2030. That outlook comes from an interested participant and depends on spending, adoption and how quickly production expands.

## Why more supply may take time to reach buyers

A larger wafer allocation to advanced memory cannot instantly ease every bottleneck. Conversely, a new packaging line cannot help a supplier that is short of usable dies. Commitments to major buyers also shape who gets the first units as output grows. This is why announcements of factories, new generations of memory and multiyear orders can all be true while customers still wait.

Watch for two signs together: confirmed output of working stacks and evidence that enough completed packages are reaching system suppliers. Those are better indicators of relief for the AI market than the value of a construction plan alone.
